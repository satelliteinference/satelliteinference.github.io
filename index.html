01 / WHY NOW

AI needs power.
Orbit has sunlight.

AI hardware already runs in space. The open questions are reliability, the cost of each step and which customers come first.

ON EARTH

Power is the bottleneck.

Data centers used about 415 TWh of electricity in 2024. The International Energy Agency's base case reaches around 945 TWh by 2030, and new AI capacity increasingly waits for generation and grid connections.

Source: International Energy Agency (IEA), Energy and AI (April 2025). Accessed October 2026.
IN ORBIT

Accelerators already fly.

Embedded GPUs already process data on satellites. In November 2025 a data-center-class GPU reached orbit, and by December it was running AI models. Several companies now plan commercial orbital compute.

Sources: Starcloud-1 mission updates (November and December 2025); SpaceX and Blue Origin FCC filings (2026). Accessed October 2026.
THE ECONOMIC GATE

Three numbers decide it.

Launch price to the operating orbit, spacecraft cost per watt and mass per kilowatt. Orbit moves closer to ground cost only as all three fall together, so each of our steps has to be useful on its own.

02 / THE ORBIT

Ride the line between
day and night.

A dawn–dusk sun-synchronous orbit follows Earth's terminator, so a solar array faces the Sun almost all year. Eclipses are seasonal and short: in our model, under 20 minutes, for about three months a year.

That is roughly 1.4 to 1.5 times the sunlight of the low orbits most satellites use today, which is why our future nodes are designed around it.

04 / THE ECONOMICS, PLAINLY

Orbit costs more
than the ground today.
It competes on speed
and capacity, not price.

Our cost model prices the three numbers that decide the cost of computing in orbit: launch to the operating orbit, the satellite's cost per watt and its mass per kilowatt. It then compares a GPU-hour in orbit with the same GPUs in a US data centre.

Launch is falling fastest. Google's Project Suncatcher study projects launch prices below $200/kg around 2035; at that price, launching a satellite costs roughly what powering an equivalent data centre on the ground does, per kilowatt-year. Our model agrees on launch, then adds the satellite and the chips, which that comparison leaves out: an orbital GPU-hour still costs about 1.5 times a terrestrial one.

Speed comes first: data that is already in space becomes results on board and reaches users sooner, with less downlink. Capacity comes next. On the ground, power has become the bottleneck: the IEA estimates that around one in five planned data-centre projects could be delayed by grid constraints, and new transmission lines take four to eight years in advanced economies. In orbit, each node brings its own power; the limit there is launch.

Each larger step, Node-1 and then clusters, needs its own funding and goes ahead only when launch and spacecraft prices are contracted rather than forecast.

06 / THE DEVELOPMENT ROADMAP

Pathfinder first.
Capacity follows evidence.

Target windows, conditional on technical results, host access, customer demand and new financing at each stage.

  1. 2026–27 / PATHFINDER

    Runtime and SI-P1.

    Develop the software, pursue Orbit-0 on an existing orbital GPU and prepare flight-ready pathfinder hardware.

    Gate: host access, qualification evidence and flight funding
  2. 2028 / FLIGHT TARGET

    SI-P1 in orbit.

    Demonstrate inference on the hosted payload and evaluate the mission with customers.

    Gate: host manifest and a separately funded flight
  3. 2029–30 / NODE-1

    Dedicated solar compute.

    Develop the first 10–20 kW SI Node around a purchased spacecraft bus and a defined customer workload. Node-1 flies alone; the 100 kW node modeled on our power, heat and economics pages is the building block of later clusters.

    Gate: pathfinder evidence and dedicated project capital
  4. 2031–35 / SCALE TARGET

    From MW to 100 MW-class.

    Target an initial MW-class cluster in 2031–32, with a longer-term ambition of 100 MW-class capacity by 2035.

    Gate: launch capacity, economics and customer commitments

Launch-agnostic by design: a 20 kW node is planned at under a tonne, within rideshare and medium-lift capacity. Cluster economics improve sharply with heavy-lift prices, and only the largest clusters need heavy-lift vehicles.
