PRODUCTS / DEVELOPMENT ROADMAP

Fly the first computer.
Learn from the mission.

Our program starts with spacecraft inference software on a ready-made flight computer hosted on a partner spacecraft, then uses flight evidence to guide dedicated solar compute nodes.

01 / THE PRODUCT FAMILY

Software and hardware.
One staged program.

SI RUNTIME

Inference within mission limits.

Software intended to run approved AI workloads on compatible spacecraft accelerators. The host operator retains command authority. Reliability and hardware portability are targets for validation.

FIRST FLIGHT

A flight computer we own.

A space-rated NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin unit, bought ready-made rather than built, flies as a hosted payload on a partner spacecraft. We own the computer and sell its compute time; the host supplies power, cooling and the link to the ground. SI-P1, a payload of our own built around an NVIDIA Jetson Thor module, follows as the next computer we own.

SI NODE

Dedicated solar compute.

Module-1, our first 20 kW compute module, comes first, ideally hosted on a partner's high-power satellite. Node-1, our first own satellite, follows with about 100 kW from five 20 kW modules in a dawn–dusk orbit, and later clusters are built from the same node. Each step requires its own development and financing.

CLUSTERS

Capacity built from nodes.

A longer-term program to aggregate orbital compute. Architecture and deployment depend on demonstrated economics, available launches and contracted demand.

These are development plans. Hardware selection and qualification follow the workload, host interface and measured power, thermal and radiation performance. Vendors, hosts and launch providers are named once agreements are signed.

02 / THE FIRST FLIGHT

The first flight.
A ready-made computer on a partner spacecraft.

We buy a flight computer that its vendor already rates for low Earth orbit, develop our runtime on its engineering unit and hand the flight unit to a host for integration. The host powers it in sessions, caps its power and carries a small daily data allowance for it.

The flight targets late 2027, on a host's mission. The order for the flight unit and the hosting agreement are the first milestones of the plan. See one day of the flight.

What the flight
has to show.

That jobs stay inside the host's power budget, carry on across power cuts and radiation faults, and come down as signed results. And what the computer draws, how warm it runs and which faults it meets in orbit: the measurements that size Module-1.

SI-P1, a payload of our own built around an NVIDIA Jetson Thor module, follows the first flight. If no slot is contracted for 2027, SI-P1 flies first, in 2028.

04 / LONGER-TERM CAPACITY TARGETS

Follow the evidence.
Expand the useful capacity.

  1. LATE 2028

    Module-1

    Our first 20 kW compute module, ideally hosted on a partner's high-power satellite, following evidence from the first flight and separate financing.

  2. 2029–30

    Node-1

    Our first own satellite: about 100 kW of compute from five 20 kW modules, developed as a separately financed program.

  3. 2031

    First 1 MW cluster

    12 nodes, 10 computing and 2 spares, built only when launch access, infrastructure costs and customer needs support it.

  4. BY 2035

    1 GW target

    The plan's target, built in stages only when launch and satellite costs cross our thresholds, and requiring substantial new capital, fleet deployment and customer commitments. At the node mass of our 2035 design, 1 GW is about 17,300 tonnes in orbit: about 250 launches of a 69-tonne Starship-class payload, before spare nodes and shielding.

  5. ECONOMIC GATE

    Three costs must improve together

    Launch cost to the operating orbit, spacecraft cost per watt and mass per kilowatt define the scale economics.

Future nodes are modeled in a dawn–dusk sun-synchronous orbit with approximately 96% annual sunlight. Seasonal eclipses remain, and the chosen orbit requires mission-specific validation. The first flight, SI-P1 and Module-1 fly in the orbits of their hosts.

The program is launch-agnostic. Module-1 is planned to fly on a partner's satellite, and a 100 kW node, 3.48 t with propellant, is within medium-lift capacity. Cluster economics improve sharply with heavy-lift prices, and only the largest clusters need heavy-lift vehicles.
